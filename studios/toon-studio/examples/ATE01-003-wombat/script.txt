This animal makes cubes inside a soft, round gut. Wombat droppings can have flat faces and distinct corners, which sounds less like biology and more like somebody installed a tiny packaging machine. Naturally, the obvious question is: where's the mold?
Let's investigate our imaginary cube factory. A square opening would be a wonderfully simple explanation. Push soft material through the right shape, and out comes a block. Except researchers found the shape developing before the material even reached the exit.
The important machinery is farther inside, in the final section of the intestine. That changes the puzzle completely. We're no longer asking how a square doorway makes a square package. We're asking how the walls of a soft tube help produce corners.
Those walls don't have identical mechanical properties all the way around. Researchers examined differences in the tissue and tested models of how varying stiffness, together with contractions, could shape the material passing through. Different parts of the wall respond differently.
Picture a simplified tube with some sections that give more easily than others. When it contracts, the material doesn't have to deform equally in every direction. The model helps explain how a rounded starting shape can develop flatter sides and more defined edges.
That picture is an explanation, not an actual look inside a working wombat. And it doesn't prove why cube-shaped droppings evolved. The useful discovery here is about the physical process: the shape is being made inside the gut, rather than stamped on at the end.
So our imaginary factory can throw away its square cookie cutter. The surprising part isn't just the cube. It's that differences within a soft tube can help build the corners before anything comes out.
